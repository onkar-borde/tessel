What does the evidence say about sleep and memory consolidation?

The evidence is strongest for a division of labour between sleep stages. Slow-wave sleep supports declarative memory consolidation1, while REM sleep appears more important for procedural and emotional memory.

Effect sizes

A recent meta-analysis found effect size d = 0.62 across 14 studies2 comparing sleep with wake after learning, with moderate heterogeneity between paradigms.

Mechanisms

Coupling between slow oscillations and spindles is the leading account. Higher sleep spindle density after learning predicts better retention the next day, although one large study did not replicate the association in older adults.

Open questions remain about causality: most human data are correlational, and targeted memory reactivation during sleep is the most promising causal tool.
